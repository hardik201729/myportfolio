The short version

Students didn't need more information. They needed information they could trust. We narrowed scope to 3 countries and 4 intents, grounded every answer on official sources, measured wrong answers before launch, and routed high-intent students to counsellors. Result: 10K+ students, leads, and an acquisition.

01

Context

University Living is a student-housing marketplace. Its users are Indian students planning to study in the UK, Australia or Ireland. Housing is the last step of that journey. Before it come dozens of questions: which course, which intake, how much money to show for the visa, whether a part-time job is allowed.

Those questions were being answered somewhere else. Mostly on Reddit, YouTube and by education agents paid on commission. By the time a student reached us for housing, someone else had already earned their trust.

The ask from leadership was short: “Can we put ChatGPT on the website?”

02

The problem behind the problem

I ran 24 interviews with students at different stages and read 300+ forum threads. The pattern was clear. Students weren't short of answers. They were drowning in conflicting ones, and the cost of a wrong answer was a rejected visa.

The job wasn't “answer questions”. It was “give me an answer I can bet my visa on.”

That changed what we would build. A general chatbot that answered everything would be impressive in a demo and dangerous in production. We needed fewer topics, answered with sources, and a clear handoff when the bot wasn't sure.

03

Scoping: saying no to most of it

I sorted 1,100 real questions from forms, WhatsApp and counsellor notes into intents, then ranked them by volume and by how bad a wrong answer would be.

FAQ pages + searchZero AI riskStudents already ignored our FAQsNo
04

What we built

Every answer went through the same path. Retrieval came first, generation second, and the guardrails decided whether the student saw the answer or a counsellor did.

Decisions that mattered

  • Sources on every answer. If the model couldn't cite an official page, it didn't answer. This one rule did more for trust than any prompt tweak.
  • “I don't know” as a feature. Low-confidence answers became a counsellor handoff with the chat attached, which turned the bot's weakness into our lead pipeline.
  • PII guardrails. Students paste passport numbers and bank balances. We scrubbed them before anything reached the model or the logs.
  • Freshness. Visa rules change. We re-indexed official sources weekly and showed a “last checked” date.
05

Evals before launch

I wrote a 300-question eval set before writing the final prompt. Each question had a reference answer checked by a counsellor. Every prompt or retrieval change ran against it, and nothing shipped if the wrong-answer rate went up.

What I'd tell another PMA demo shows the AI can be right. An eval set shows how often it's wrong. Write the eval first.

07

What I'd do differently

  • Instrument the handoff earlier. For the first 3 weeks we couldn't tell which chats became bookings, and that's the number leadership cared about most.
  • Ship WhatsApp first. Students live there, and web usage was lower than the WhatsApp pilot.
  • Show the eval score inside the team's weekly review, so quality is everyone's metric, not just mine.
